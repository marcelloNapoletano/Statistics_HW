HMWK 6 · Riemann vs Lebesgue, and the normal CDF

Two ways of slicing an integral, tested on classical and pathological functions; and how to compute the cumulative distribution function of the normal distribution when no elementary formula exists.

Two ways of measuring the area under a curve

Imagine a pile of coins on a table and you want its total value. Riemann goes through the table from left to right, in slices of equal width, and adds up what he finds in each slice ("partition the domain"). Lebesgue first sorts the coins by value, then asks, for each value, "how much table is covered by coins of this value?" ("partition the range and measure the preimages"). For nice functions the two answers agree; the interesting part is when they do not.

📏 Riemann: vertical strips

Cut [a, b] into N strips. On each strip the function has a smallest and a largest value: the lower sum uses the smallest, the upper sum the largest. If the two squeeze to the same number as N grows, f is Riemann-integrable.

🧱 Lebesgue: horizontal layers

Cut the values into K layers of height Δy. The set where f lies in layer j is the preimage Ej; its length μ(Ej) is its measure. Sum: Σ (height of layer) × μ(Ej). Lower and upper sums always differ by just Δy·(b−a): they squeeze for every bounded measurable f.

⚡ Where they differ

Riemann needs f to be "mostly continuous". Lebesgue only needs to measure sets, so it handles the Dirichlet function, wild sets and unbounded functions. In practice, however, measuring preimages is only easy when you know them.

🧭 Comparison: accuracy, convergence, efficiency

🎯 Accuracy and convergence

For smooth functions the Riemann midpoint rule is much better: its error falls like 1/N², while Lebesgue sums with a plain level value fall like 1/K (using the middle of each layer improves it to about 1/K²). At a jump, Riemann falls only like 1/N, whereas Lebesgue with levels that hit the two values is exact. For functions with infinitely many oscillations or dense discontinuities, the Lebesgue squeeze keeps working while Riemann's stops.

⏱️ Computational efficiency

Riemann needs only N evaluations of f. Lebesgue needs the measure of the preimage of each level. If it is known analytically (the exact version) the cost is K evaluations of a simple formula, but this is rarely the case. If it must be measured by sampling f at n points, one has to sort the values (n log n) and, curiously, with K = n layers the result is identical to the Riemann sum with its terms put in order. See the "Efficiency" button.

🌪️ Pathological cases

Dirichlet: Riemann's sums depend on the chosen points (1 with rational points, 0 with irrational points), Lebesgue gives 0. Thomae: Riemann-integrable with integral 0, although discontinuous at every rational. Fat Cantor set: lower sums 0, upper sums → ½, Lebesgue ½. Cantor staircase: continuous, so both work, but its derivative is 0 almost everywhere.

The standard normal CDF and why it is hard

The standard normal CDF is the area under the bell curve up to x: Φ(x) = ∫−∞x φ(t) dt, with φ(t) = e−t²/2/√(2π). You would like a formula like "Φ(x) = some combination of sin, exp, log, powers…", but there is none. The sections below explain why, what the error function does about it, and compare several ways of computing Φ(x) on a computer.

🚫 No elementary formula (Liouville)

Liouville (1835) proved: if ∫ f·eg dx (f, g rational functions) can be written with elementary functions, then it equals R·eg for some rational function R. For the bell curve f = 1, g = −x², so we would need R with (R·e−x²)' = e−x², i.e. R' − 2xR = 1.

That is impossible: if R is a polynomial of degree d ≥ 0, then 2xR has degree d+1 ≥ 1 and R' is smaller, so the left side is not the constant 1; if R has a pole of order m, R' has a pole of order m+1 and nothing cancels it. Hence ∫e−x²dx is not elementary, and neither is Φ (a rescaled version of it).

🧩 The error function

Mathematicians simply give the integral a name: erf(z) = (2/√π) ∫0z e−t² dt. Then, exactly,

Φ(x) = ½ · [ 1 + erf( x / √2 ) ]

This is an exact analytical representation, but erf is only a name for the unsolved integral: it tells us what Φ is, not how to compute it. A computer still needs an algorithm.

🛠️ Methods compared here

Numerical integration of φ on [0, x] (trapezoid, Simpson); series: Maclaurin series of erf (alternating signs, with the cancellation trouble the HMC notes warn about) and the equivalent series with positive terms; a continued fraction for the tails; the classic Abramowitz–Stegun approximation of erf; and a hybrid (series near the centre, continued fraction in the tails) that reaches full double precision.

Symmetry Φ(−x) = 1 − Φ(x) lets every method work with x ≥ 0 only.

🧾 Conclusions

🎯 Accuracy

Trapezoid is slow (error ∝ 1/n²), Simpson much faster (1/n⁴) but both need hundreds of evaluations for 10⁻¹⁰. The alternating erf series is excellent near 0 but explodes for large x: terms of size ex²/2 are added and subtracted and the digits are lost. The positive-term series avoids this; the continued fraction is excellent exactly where the series is slow, in the tails; the Abramowitz–Stegun formula is a cheap 7-digit approximation.

⏱️ Efficiency

The hybrid method needs 10 to 60 terms for full double precision, with relative accuracy even for tails of size 10⁻¹⁵: the winner in accuracy per unit of work. Integration rules cost 100–10,000 evaluations of exp for 3–9 digits; A–S needs a single exp but stops at 10⁻⁷.

🔢 Why numerics are still needed

Writing Φ with erf is exact but only moves the problem: erf is itself an integral without elementary form. Every practical implementation (in R, Python, C…) uses series, continued fractions and rational approximations, chosen region by region, exactly like the hybrid here.
