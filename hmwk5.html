HMWK 5 · Welford's recurrences and sampling estimates

How to compute the mean and the variance of a stream of scores in a single pass, and what the sampling mean and sampling variance of m hackers tell us about the true μ and σ².

Exercise 1 · Welford's recurrence formulas

Goal: compute the mean and the variance of a stream of numbers one value at a time, without storing the data and without reading it twice. Knuth's formula does it for the mean; Welford's does it for the sum of squares (SS) and hence the variance. Notation: after k values, Mk is the mean and SSk = Σ (xi − Mk)² the sum of squared deviations from that mean.

① The mean (Knuth)

The sum of the first k values is k·Mk = (k−1)·Mk−1 + xk. Divide by k and rearrange:

Mk = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1) / k

The new mean is the old one, moved towards the new value by a fraction 1/k of the gap.

② The sum of squares (Welford)

Expanding the square gives SSk = Σ xi² − k·Mk². Subtract the same formula for k−1:

SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − k·Mk² + (k−1)·Mk−1²

Since (k−1)·Mk−1 = k·Mk − xk, the last term is (k·Mk − xk)·Mk−1, so

= xk(xk − Mk−1) − k·Mk(Mk − Mk−1)

By ①, k(Mk − Mk−1) = xk − Mk−1, hence the difference is (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk):

SSk = SSk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk)

Start from M0 = 0, SS0 = 0. The "old gap" times the "new gap" is never negative, so SS can only grow.

③ The variance

The sampling variance divides by k−1 (unbiased), the "plug-in" variance by k:

sk² = SSk / (k − 1)    (k ≥ 2)

Because Mk − xk = −(1 − 1/k)(xk − Mk−1), one also gets a direct recurrence for the variance:

sk² = (k−2)/(k−1) · sk−1² + (xk − Mk−1)² / k

The code. The core of the algorithm is three lines inside the loop:

M = SS = 0
for k, x in enumerate(data, 1):      # Python  (the JavaScript used in this page is identical)
    d = x - M          # gap from the OLD mean
    M += d / k         # Knuth: new mean
    SS += d * (x - M)  # Welford: (x - M_old) * (x - M_new)
variance = SS / (k - 1)

🧪 Try it on your own numbers

Type numbers separated by commas or spaces. The table shows the three recurrences step by step and checks the final result against the textbook two-pass formula.

Why use it? The "textbook" shortcut SS = Σx² − k·M² subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers when the data are large and close together, and loses all its digits. Welford never does.

Exercise 2 · Sampling mean and variance of m hackers

This continues HMWK 4. Now every attempt gets a score between 0 and 10, drawn from a fixed distribution that you choose (its true mean μ and variance σ² are computed exactly from the probabilities). m hackers each attack n systems, so every hacker collects n scores. For each hacker the page runs Welford's recurrences along the n scores and obtains the hacker's sampling mean x̄ and sampling variance s². Then it averages the m sampling means and the m sampling variances and compares them with μ and σ². Everything is done for many values of n at once, so you can see what changes when each hacker attacks more systems.

💬 Conclusions

1 · The averages hit the targets

The average of the m sampling means is on μ and the average of the m sampling variances (with n−1) is on σ², for every n, even n = 2. The differences you see are only random noise of size σ/√(nm) and shrink when m grows. We say that x̄ and s² are unbiased estimators.

Dividing SS by n instead of n−1 gives an average of σ²·(n−1)/n: too small, noticeably so for small n (button "Mean of the s²").

2 · Why this matters in inference

In practice μ and σ² are unknown and we have one data set: one x̄ and one s². Unbiasedness tells us that, over many possible data sets, these numbers are right on average, so they are legitimate estimates. Moreover s² can replace σ² in the standard error s/√n, which is what confidence intervals and tests need. Welford's recurrences compute both online, in one pass.

3 · What changes with n

Both estimators become more precise: the spread of x̄ across hackers is σ/√n, the spread of s² is √((μ₄ − σ⁴)/n) (μ₄ is the 4th central moment): both shrink like 1/√n, i.e. four times more systems halve the error. The distributions of x̄ and s² narrow around μ and σ² and become bell-shaped (Central Limit Theorem). This is consistency. Increasing m, instead, does not make each hacker more precise: it only makes our picture of the averages more precise.
