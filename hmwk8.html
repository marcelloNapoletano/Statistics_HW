HMWK 8 · Modes of convergence, and Bayesian updating

What "converges" can mean for random sequences (and what each meaning does in the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem), and how Bayes' theorem turns prior beliefs and data into posterior beliefs.

Four ways a sequence of random numbers can "settle down"

Think of Xₙ as the average of the first n coin flips. It is random, and it changes with every n. What does it mean that Xₙ "tends to" 0.5? There are four different answers, from the strictest to the most relaxed. They are not the same, and different theorems use different ones.

📏 Mean-square (L²)

E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0. "On average the squared error vanishes." Easy to compute (for a mean: σ²/n). It forces convergence in probability.

🎯 In probability

For every ε > 0: P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0. "The chance of being off by more than ε shrinks." At each fixed n the bad event may still happen, but it gets rarer. This is the weak law of large numbers.

🧵 Almost surely (a.s.)

P(Xₙ → X) = 1. "Follow one experiment forever: its numbers converge, with probability 1." Equivalent to P(supm≥n|Xₘ − X| > ε) → 0. This is the strong law of large numbers.

🔔 In distribution

Fₙ(x) → F(x) at every continuity point of F. "The shape of the histogram converges" – not the values themselves. This is the central limit theorem.

How they are linked. a.s. ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution, and L² ⇒ in probability. Nothing else holds in general (see the counterexamples below), except that in distribution to a constant is the same as in probability.

Rule of thumb: the LLN says where the average goes (a number); the CLT says how it fluctuates around it (a bell curve of width σ/√n). The LLN uses the strong modes (probability, a.s., L²), the CLT the weakest one (distribution), which is enough to compute probabilities such as confidence intervals.

√ Why √n shows up everywhere

➕ Variances add

For independent (even just uncorrelated) variables, Var(X₁ + … + Xₙ) = nσ². The variance grows like n, so the spread (standard deviation) grows like √n, not like n. Errors partly cancel: some are positive, some negative.

➗ Averaging shrinks it

Dividing by n: sd(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n. To halve the error you need four times the data. This is also the speed of the LLN in L²: E[(X̄ₙ − μ)²] = σ²/n.

🎚️ The only right zoom

X̄ₙ − μ is of size 1/√n. Multiply by n and it blows up (sd ≍ √n); multiply by nothing and it collapses to 0. Multiplying by √n gives something of size σ, a stable shape: the normal curve of the CLT. The same √n appears in standard errors, confidence intervals, test statistics and "√n-consistent" estimators.

🔗 Why independence matters

With correlation ρ between all pairs, Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²(ρ + (1−ρ)/n). It no longer goes to 0, it stalls at σ²ρ: no LLN, no √n. With identical copies (ρ = 1), averaging does nothing at all. Try it in the "Spread vs n" view.

Updating what we believe when new data arrive

Probability is not only about dice; it also measures how strongly we believe something. Conditional probability P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) is "the probability of A once we know B happened". Bayes' theorem turns it around: from P(data | hypothesis), which is easy to compute, to P(hypothesis | data), which is what we really want.

P(θ | data) = P(data | θ) · P(θ) / P(data)   ⇒   posterior ∝ likelihood × prior

🧠 Prior P(θ)

What we believed before seeing the data: from earlier studies, physics, or honest ignorance (a flat prior). It is a probability distribution over the unknown θ.

📊 Likelihood P(data | θ)

How well each value of θ explains the data we saw, seen as a function of θ. It is not a probability distribution over θ (it does not integrate to 1). Frequentist statistics (maximum likelihood) uses it alone.

🎯 Posterior P(θ | data)

What we believe after: prior reweighted by likelihood and renormalised by P(data) = ∫ P(data | θ)P(θ)dθ. Today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior, so data can be absorbed one batch at a time.

In statistics, "conditional" also appears as P(F | X) (a conditional frequency, as in HMWK 7), and as the conditional distribution of a model, P(data | θ). Bayesians treat θ itself as random, because it is uncertain to us.

🧪 Three worked examples

1 · A test result (discrete prior)

Prior: 1% of cases are real. Likelihood: the test is positive for 95% of real cases and for 5% of non-cases. Posterior: P(D | +) = 0.95·0.01 / (0.95·0.01 + 0.05·0.99) ≈ 16%, not 95%. Most positives come from the huge healthy group: the base-rate fallacy. The same maths rules intrusion detectors and spam filters.

2 · A proportion (Beta–Binomial)

Prior p ~ Beta(a, b); data k successes in n trials. Posterior is Beta(a + k, b + n − k): the prior acts like a + b imaginary earlier trials. The posterior mean (a + k)/(a + b + n) is a weighted mean of the prior mean and the observed frequency k/n, with weights (a + b) and n.

3 · A mean (Normal–Normal)

Prior μ ~ N(μ₀, τ²), data x̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n). Posterior is normal with precision (1/variance) = 1/τ² + n/σ² and a precision-weighted mean of μ₀ and x̄: the estimate is shrunk from the data towards the prior, more so when data are few or noisy.

⚖️ Advantages, limits, and influence on conclusions

✅ Advantages

Direct answers ("p lies in [0.5, 0.8] with probability 95%"). Uses prior knowledge and small data sensibly. Coherent sequential updating. Works with complicated models (hierarchical, missing data). Gives full uncertainty, not only a point estimate.

⚠️ Limitations

The prior must be chosen, and it is partly subjective. A flat prior is not "neutral" for every transformation of θ (Jeffreys' prior fixes this for a proportion). The normalising integral can be hard (MCMC and approximations). A very strong wrong prior can mislead for a long time.

🌊 Prior vs data

With little data the prior dominates; with a lot of data the likelihood dominates and different priors give nearly the same posterior (Bernstein–von Mises). Check this: compare posteriors for several priors and see whether the conclusion changes. If it does, the data alone are not enough.

🆚 Credible vs confidence

A 95% credible interval says "given the data, θ is in it with probability 95%". A 95% confidence interval says "the procedure catches θ in 95% of repetitions". With a flat prior and lots of data they nearly coincide.
